# Concept and faithfulness

Read this when a visual reference, decorative identity, story, or recognizable subject materially governs the design.

## Establish reference authority

Record which source governs each property rather than treating every reference as universally authoritative. Separate:

- overall composition, proportions, and principal view;
- focal sculpture, anatomy, pose, or motif;
- surface language and ornament;
- color, material, and lighting intent;
- dimensional and functional requirements;
- later user clarifications.

A generated concept establishes visible intent, not reliable hidden geometry. Labels or pictured hardware become requirements only through the user's instructions or measurements. A later focused reference may replace one element without replacing the whole design.

## Brief and blockout

Translate the idea into observable constraints:

- protected usable interior and contents;
- external planning envelope;
- focal point and storytelling moment;
- primary viewing angles and important secondary views;
- silhouette, proportions, hierarchy, and negative space;
- repeated motifs and characteristic shape language;
- openings, contacts, supports, and motion;
- areas that may adapt and areas whose identity must be preserved.

Build a complete low-cost blockout before decorative detail. Show actual structural geometry and clearly labeled reserves for later sculpture, hardware, effects, or electronics. Judge the whole object before polishing isolated pieces.

For recognizable organic elements, establish anatomy, gesture, attachments, and major folds before texture. Review actual geometry with neutral materials and multiple views; dramatic lighting and color can conceal form errors.

## Faithfulness audit

Compare the authoritative concept with renders of the actual current model. Use matched viewpoints or silhouette overlays when practical, plus diagnostic views for hidden contacts. Do not rely only on the most attractive presentation render.

Review:

- overall silhouette and proportion;
- focal point and visual hierarchy;
- character, pose, gesture, and anatomy;
- scale and placement relationships;
- negative space and sightlines;
- decorative motifs and shape language;
- material and surface character;
- color and lighting intent where relevant;
- important story details;
- visibility from intended viewing angles.

Distinguish literal fidelity from intent fidelity. A changed footprint may require recomposition rather than uniform scaling. Preserve the relationships that create the design's identity and explain why a literal copy would fail.

## Compromise register

Record every visually meaningful departure:

| Field | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Concept property | What the governing reference intends |
| Modeled change | What differs in the current design |
| Classification | Required compromise, chosen adaptation, accidental drift, unresolved ambiguity, or faithful as modeled |
| Reason | Strength, process, assembly, safety, protected function, user preference, or other evidence |
| Visible effect | What the user will notice and from which views |
| Alternatives | Credible options and their visual, functional, cost, or risk tradeoffs |
| Recommendation | Builder's recommended choice without treating it as approval |
| User decision | Accepted, rejected, revised, or pending |

Manufacturing constraints justify proposing a departure; they do not automatically authorize it. Present a compromise before proceeding when it materially affects identity, focal silhouette, decorative character, story, or an explicitly protected feature.

If repeated revisions do not converge, stop guessing. Inspect any existing detail reference; otherwise ask for the smallest useful crop or clarification showing the disputed feature with enough neighboring context to establish contact, direction, and scale. Continue independent work while leaving that area stable.
